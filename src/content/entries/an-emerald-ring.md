## Dinosaurs!

When I was a child, like many others, I was really into dinosaurs. I had toys, books, DVDs, and virtually everything dino-related. I think that's pretty common among children, but this may well have been my first real obsession. I was the kind of kid who, at birthday parties, would sit staring at dirt and rocks instead of playing with the other kids.

One day, my parents gave me a hammer and a chisel as a gift. Yeah. For me, that was the best gift ever, because it meant I could finally split open — literally — a whole new world. I'd sit outside for hours, splitting open any rock I could find that looked remotely interesting.

Most of them were just rocks, but I did eventually find a couple of fossils: a tiny crab and a crinoid.

The more rocks I split open, the more I noticed. I was fascinated by the patterns and the uniqueness of each one, details that many people would probably overlook. Over time, that fascination grew into an interest in rocks as a whole, rather than just dinosaurs and fossils.

And so my rock collection began! At first, it was just any remotely cool-looking rock I could find, but eventually it grew more sophisticated, with fossils, minerals, gemstones, and so on.

Some notable pieces include various emeralds, a few diamonds and rubies, a sapphire, a T. rex tooth, raptor teeth, a piece of ankylosaur armour, a Triceratops bone, and more. Actually, let me quickly grab one to show, here:

<img src="../../assets/entries/an-emerald-ring/fossil.png" width="40%" style="border-radius: 16px;" />

There are plenty of other things in the collection, of course, but those are some of the coolest. I still add to it every now and then, and to this day, it's still here.

As I grew into my teens, computers and technology took over, and that original childhood spark got buried.

And that's where today's story begins.

## Digging Up the Past

For the past year or two, I've spent a lot of time trying to figure out who I am, as I imagine many people my age do. Part of that has involved reconnecting with the childhood interests that made me happy.

A year ago, that was rocks.

I spent hours every week reading about anything and everything rock-related. One evening, I looked over at my collection and remembered how easy it was to buy rocks, fossils, and gemstones online. I did exactly that as a kid, buying gems, minerals, and fossils from certified sellers. That's how much of my collection had grown in the first place.

The first marketplace I visited was for fossils, and, like a kid in a candy store, I went a little crazy. I almost bought a mammoth rib on impulse, for whatever reason:

And I mean, come on. If I were a comically rich, evil CEO sitting in an office at the top of a skyscraper, I would 100% fill the place with weird random shit like this. I stand by that.

In any case, after looking at fossils for a while, I switched over to gemstones, something I definitely haven't talked about enough yet, given the subject of this entry.

I ended up on a website called [Gem Select](https://www.gemselect.com/), which sells all kinds of loose gemstones. I can highly recommend browsing it if you're a collector or an individual buyer like me. It's a bit of a rabbit hole.

My favourite gemstone is emerald. And I know, some gemstone nerd will tell you that sapphire is ten times better, or that tsavorite garnet is just an emerald but better, but it's an emerald. Come on.

So, I went on a bit of a shopping spree, as one does at 2 a.m.

## Now What?

Well, I could just put them on display and mostly forget about them, apart from an occasional glance, but that felt a little sad.

So, being the perfectly reasonable person that I am, I had an idea: *Jewellery.*

And all it took was a [YouTube video](https://youtu.be/nnzWZN30K_I).

In the video, Mike (the YouTuber) spends a day with a professional jeweller, trying to make an engagement ring to propose to his girlfriend with.

Now, I'm not trying to get engaged, but I loved the idea, the experience, and especially the way the video approached the whole thing as a project.

Also, shout-out to Mike. I love his mindset and the way he keeps trying new things in his life. He's a genuine inspiration to me.

As for what to make, I had a few ideas:

- Make a necklace with the ruby for my mum
- Make an emerald ring for myself
- Make an emerald ring for my other half at the time

My mum's favourite stone is ruby, but this particular one is fracture-filled. That makes it substantially cheaper while largely preserving its appearance, but the trade-off is durability. I didn't want to risk using it in something that might get knocked around, and even making a necklace felt like a significant challenge for a first attempt. I still plan to make something like that for her one day, perhaps with a better stone and a bit more budget.

I only considered making a ring for myself because, well, it's an emerald, and I thought that would be cool. Truth be told, I'm not much of an accessories guy. If you saw me on the street, I'd probably look like John Doe. So I quickly scrapped that idea.

That left one option.

I really liked the idea, too. Her birthday was coming up, and I thought the particular shade of emerald would suit her perfectly.

I had two emeralds. One was the obvious choice: it was higher-quality and clearer. But I actually preferred the other one. It had a distinct, earthy aesthetic that I really liked: a kind of muted, ivy-green colour. I think that was one of my favourite shades for her.

So I decided to go in that direction. After a healthy amount of Pinterest browsing, I came across this image:

Obviously, my emerald was tiny compared with this stone, and the one in the picture isn't even an emerald. What caught my eye was the ring itself. I loved the branch-like design and the imperfections in the band. The colour worked really well, too; it wasn't the usual boring yellow gold. I thought it was a strong starting point.

Then I waited.

The gemstones are quite small, yes, but that's ideal for jewellery.

I think it's also time to address the elephant in the room: the cost.

You might expect these gemstones to be pretty expensive, but that's not necessarily the case. For instance, one of the emeralds cost me just €60, and one of the diamonds only €20. Of course, gemstone prices vary enormously depending on size, quality, treatments, and so on. But I think people often overestimate the cost of individual stones because they're used to seeing them in finished jewellery, where you're also paying for the metal, design, and craftsmanship. So no, I'm not some secret millionaire, although that doesn't mean this particular shopping spree was cheap for me.

Anyway, now that I had the gems, I started brainstorming: how was I actually going to make the ring?

The design I found was complicated. It would probably require a mould and a casting process, followed by plenty of filing, sanding, and polishing. The first thing I looked into was workshops like the one in Mike's video, where an experienced jeweller guides you through making a ring. The problem was that these workshops usually kept the designs quite basic and limited. I wanted something slightly more ambitious.

So I started looking for other jewellers. I knew it would cost money, but by this point I was far too invested in the idea to stop. In other words, I was finding every possible excuse to keep going.

## The Hunt Begins

Alright, so now all that stood between me and the ring was finding someone crazy enough to let me work alongside them without charging an equally crazy amount of money.

I did the only reasonable thing: I took the emerald with me and went door to door, visiting every jeweller in Amsterdam I could find until someone said yes.

I am not kidding when I say this took me two months.

I genuinely felt like the craziest person ever. Some of the reactions I got were really getting to me at first, but looking back, I appreciate the experience. It pushed me so far outside my comfort zone that I came away feeling much more confident in myself.

After two months, I finally found a jeweller who said yes. I still remember the rush of adrenaline I felt when it happened. There was one problem, though: the design would have to be relatively simple. The branch idea was a no-go. At that point, I was desperate enough to agree.

There were a few other conditions: I'd have to pay for the metal, show up at the times they chose, and assume the risks involved. Otherwise, we were good to go.

That was all fine by me. I was beyond excited.

We settled on one session a week, an hour per session.

I'm still not entirely sure why they agreed. It was a family-owned business, and I had originally spoken to one of the daughters. Her father was generally the one making the jewellery, but she was the one who ended up working with me on the ring. I suspect she liked the idea and decided to give it a shot.

She was incredibly kind, and I couldn't have asked for someone better to work with.

## The First Session

A week later, I showed up for our first session. We sat down, did some planning, and, frankly, spent most of the hour talking. I learned an incredible amount from the whole experience, and a surprising amount of it came from that first conversation.

We agreed on 14-karat gold. It's a common choice for everyday jewellery, and for good reason. People sometimes assume that 18k or 24k gold must automatically be better, but there's more to consider than gold content: durability, workability, and how the piece will hold up over time all matter. Pure 24k gold is relatively soft, which makes it less practical for a ring that's going to be worn regularly. Without turning this into a metalworking essay, 14k gold is an alloy: gold mixed with other metals, often including copper and silver in yellow gold. Those additional metals make it harder and better suited to everyday wear.

We also talked about the emerald. Emeralds can be quite fragile, particularly when pressure is applied during setting. To avoid risking the stone, we agreed that she would set it herself. We'd also use a setting designed to hold the emerald securely.

I left that day feeling like it was finally happening.

One detail I remember fondly was the contrast between the front and back of the store. The front was cramped, with very little space and people constantly moving around, so it could get fairly chaotic. But the moment you stepped into the back, everything changed. It was much bigger, quieter, and full of equipment and interesting things. I found that contrast pretty fun.

## Creating the Ring

Making the ring wasn't terribly complicated. We'd settled on a simple design, so the individual steps were fairly straightforward — at least on paper.

For the band, we cut a length of round 14k gold wire, bent it around a mandrel to size, soldered the seam, and then used a small mallet to bring it into shape. This was more challenging than I expected. I'm not especially good at hands-on crafts, and the combination of fiddly movements and nerves didn't help. Still, with a little guidance, we got there. The band didn't have to be perfect yet, since we'd be filing and polishing it later.

For the head, we used a ready-made four-prong basket sized to fit the stone. Interestingly, these settings often come pre-made, either cast or die-struck to standard stone dimensions. I have no idea which kind ours was. I soldered it onto the band, which was probably the most stressful part for me. My only previous soldering experience involved a few LED-strip wires and an extremely cheap, probably dangerous soldering iron. I still remembered how much I struggled with that. Thankfully, she guided me through it, and I was genuinely proud of the result.

At that point, the band was largely finished. I remember worrying that the emerald would look too small once it was in place. I'm not sure why; I think an empty setting can be a little deceptive. Once the stone was in, though, the proportions looked spot on.

Seeing the band come together was also the first time I really felt like I made something. It reminded me of building LEGO: you work on one little piece after another, and then, suddenly, enough of it comes together that you can see the whole thing. All that time and patience finally clicks into place.

Then came the risky part: setting the emerald. As we'd agreed, she handled that herself, and thankfully it all went smoothly; I mostly just stood there in silence like I was witnessing a stressful surgery. The prongs had to be pushed over carefully, without putting too much pressure on the stone. We also had to avoid applying heat once the emerald was set. Emeralds are often treated with oil to improve their apparent clarity, and mine was no exception. Excessive heat can affect that treatment and potentially worsen existing fractures through thermal shock.

I really wish I'd taken pictures of the work in progress. That's my one regret, because now I don't have anything to show you from those sessions. I'm generally terrible at remembering to take photos. It might sound contradictory given what I've written on the journal's homepage, but I often prefer to enjoy what I'm doing rather than interrupt the moment to document it. Still, one picture would have been nice.

Finally, on 26 February, a beautiful sunny day, I had my last session. The difficult work was behind us by then; all that remained was the final polishing.

All told, the actual work took about five hours. Two months spent searching for a jeweller, for five hours of making a ring. To be fair, her birthday was getting close while I was still searching, so there was a point when I was genuinely worried I wouldn't finish in time. Somehow, everything fell into place.

## Concluding

Not long after, I gave her the ring. I'd rather keep the details of that moment private, but there's one thing I'd like to say.

Rings can carry a lot of symbolic meaning, especially in romantic relationships. This wasn't meant to be some grand statement. I wanted to make something, experience a craft I never tried before, and give the result to someone I cared about. She understood that about me. And, of course, that doesn't mean it wasn't a little special in its own way.

I might try my hand at jewellery making again someday, though I suspect this was a one-off. What mattered most to me wasn't the ring itself, but everything it took to make it. Looking back, I think this experience helped shape the attitude I have towards unusual projects nowadays, like the [Tiny Tapeout project](https://journal.lucagoddijn.com/entries/ttgf-mac-engine/). The ring was primarily something tangible to show for the whole experience: a reminder that I'd followed a strange idea all the way through and actually made it happen.
